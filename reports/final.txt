Text for the submission form: what I expect it to score and why (final recipe)
====================================================================================================
Final recipe. Score = base log-odds + 1.0 x log(partner_rate_ratio). The base is a logistic regression on 18 label-free claim and partner features; partner_rate_ratio is the partner's confirmed fraud rate since 1 May 2026 relative to the average, and is exactly 1.0 for a partner with no decided claim. It is trained on the 11,146 labelled claims up to 30 Jun 2026 (141 frauds). A decision rule I fixed before running anything chose a simpler benchmark (the ratio alone). I deviated from it and logged why: the benchmark cannot rank the claims of partners without a fraud record, and the intervals do not separate the two.

Validation. On the primary fold (June 2026: 713 claims, 22 frauds from 8 partners; fraud history frozen on 1 June and assumed known after a 14-day investigation lag) the same recipe scores PR-AUC 0.465 (90% interval 0.057 to 0.661) and ROC-AUC 0.884 (0.768 to 0.952). It finds 11 of the 22 frauds in the top 40 (0 to 18) and 15 in the top 100 (4 to 29); picking 40 blind finds about 1.2. The benchmark scores PR-AUC 0.347 and ROC-AUC 0.843 with 13 in the top 40. Intervals come from 1,000 resamples of partners with a fixed seed and are wide because 22 frauds from 8 partners is very little.

What I expect on the Jul-Sep test. A PR-AUC somewhere between about 0.16 and 0.56, with a point guess of 0.30 to 0.35. The range is computed on the June fold: 0.16 is what the label-free part alone scores (the result if partner history stops helping) and 0.56 is the full recipe when every outcome is known at once. The point guess is my judgement, and it is deliberately below the June figure of 0.465: the coefficient of 1.0, the base model (the survivor of four compared on June), the review rule and the calibration were all settled with June in view, and June is the only month I could validate on. That is selection optimism, and I cannot measure its size. A result under 0.16 would mean the fraud pattern has moved again. Accuracy is not a result I report: predicting "not fraud" for every June claim is already 96.91% accurate and catches nothing.

History and lag for the final model. History is frozen on 30 Jun 2026 using every outcome known by then (1,422 decided new-regime claims, 36 frauds), i.e. no lag at the cut-off. The export cannot reveal the real investigation lag. The test runs to 30 Sep, so for the 67% of test claims submitted from 30 July on, every June outcome would be decided even under a 30-day lag; only July claims get somewhat optimistic history. Validation keeps the 14-day lag as the conservative headline (380 decided claims, 8 frauds available); the same fold gives PR-AUC 0.561 with no lag and 0.156 with a 30-day lag, where no history is usable yet. 111 of the 2,252 test claims (4.9%) come from partners with no decided new-regime claim and are scored by the label-free part alone (under a 14-day lag it would be 236).

Why the test could score lower. (1) The history is up to 92 days stale by the end of the test; the oldest history I could validate was 43 days old. (2) The investigation lag is unknown. (3) On the stress-test fold (May 2026, the first month of the new pattern) this recipe scored ROC-AUC 0.062, far below random, as did every fitted model: it had learned the old pattern, which reversed on 1 May. Another shift would hurt the same way. (4) Everything rests on 22 frauds from 8 partners, so the model choice itself could be luck.

Why it could score higher. Fraud was still rising (1.97% of claims in May, 3.09% in June), which lifts PR-AUC. And most of the test is scored with fresher and fuller history than the validation had: 1,422 decided claims with 36 frauds against 380 with 8.

How the outlet-identity question was handled, and what it cost. The first base model had 40 features, 21 of them city and partner-type columns, and some of its largest coefficients were cities. With about ten fraudulent outlets those columns can simply memorise which outlet is which. I changed the base model three times, each time against a rule written down before the run, and then stopped. (1) Dropping city, partner type, partner age and the new-outlet flag together was rejected: the original won on PR-AUC in 99.3% of partner resamples (0.413 against 0.291). (2) Dropping only city and partner type was adopted: the original won in only 11.5% (0.413 against 0.463). (3) Checking the explanations then showed a second problem: the continuous partner-age feature had been learned mostly from the months before 1 May, when 105 of 105 confirmed frauds came from established outlets, so it raised the score of long-established outlets (by up to +0.85 in log-odds together with the flag) although after 1 May they had 2 frauds in 1,211 claims. I removed it on that principle, not because of its June score: the model with it won in only 34.8% of resamples (0.463 against 0.465), so nothing measurable was lost. My other worry, that a very low age would cancel the new-outlet flag for outlets onboarded after June, was not borne out: it shifted them by -0.12 against a flag worth +2.90. The new-outlet flag stays because the group effect is measured: 34 frauds in 211 claims after 1 May for new outlets against 2 in 1,211 for established ones. Its cost is real: it is what makes the model rank backwards when the pattern changes (ROC-AUC 0.062 on the May stress test, against 0.428 without it), and most new outlets are clean. Counted one way everywhere (new outlets = outlets onboarded on or after 1 Oct 2025 with at least one decided claim first submitted on or after 1 May 2026), there are 45 new outlets, of which 8 have a confirmed fraud and 37 have none. Explanations never name a city or a partner type, and the outlet line always carries those group numbers.

Money. A full review list of 40 does not pay on this data: in June it nets Rs -4,320 after Rs 380 per genuine claim held and Rs 260 per claim reviewed. The rule I recommend reviews only claims whose partner has confirmed fraud above the average rate: 25 reviews, 13 frauds and a net Rs 8,039 for June (-3,145 to 21,143), about Rs 322 per claim reviewed. That list is the same whichever base model is used, because it depends only on which partners have a confirmed fraud record; the base model only orders the claims in it and the claims beyond it. Its margin: the frauds it caught averaged Rs 1,469, so it breaks even when 35% of the reviewed claims are fraud, and 52% were (13 of 25; it needs 9). The rule was defined after seeing June and is not independently validated: one month, 8 partners.

Expected frauds. The scores sum to 100 over the 2,252 test claims. 60 of that sits in the 112 claims from the 10 partners with confirmed fraud above the average rate (an implied fraud rate of 53%), which is the group the review rule looks at. 30 is spread over the 2,029 claims from partners with a clean or below-average record (1.5%) and 11 over the 111 claims from partners with no decided claim yet (9.5%).

Probabilities and their limits. The scores carry a two-parameter Platt correction (slope 1.017, intercept +0.575) fitted on the June validation predictions. Before it the model was short by 6.6 frauds among the flagged partners (6.4 predicted, 13 observed) and by +0.7 among everyone else. The intercept is a single number added to every claim, so it lifts the unflagged claims as well. The result: the implied fraud rates for the two unflagged groups in the test (1.5% and 9.5%) are above what June showed for the same groups (1.1% and 1.9%), and the total of 100 is probably too high for that reason. Treat the probabilities as indicative; the ranking is what matters and what I would be judged on.
